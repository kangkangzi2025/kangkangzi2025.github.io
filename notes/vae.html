1. Introduction

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), introduced by Kingma and Welling (2014) and concurrently by Rezende et al. (2014), represent a fundamental approach to deep generative modeling. Unlike traditional autoencoders that learn deterministic encodings, VAEs learn a probabilistic latent space, enabling both powerful representation learning and principled generation of new samples.

The key insight of VAEs is to frame generative modeling as a variational inference problem. We assume data is generated by first sampling a latent variable \(z\) from a prior distribution, then generating the observation \(x\) from a conditional distribution. Learning involves simultaneously fitting the generative model and an approximate inference network.

Key Insight

VAEs unify two perspectives: (1) autoencoders with a regularized latent space, and (2) directed probabilistic graphical models with amortized variational inference. This dual interpretation makes them both practical and theoretically grounded.

Why are VAEs important? They provide:

  • Principled generative modeling: A theoretically grounded framework for learning data distributions
  • Learned representations: Latent codes that capture meaningful data structure
  • Scalability: Efficient training with stochastic gradient descent
  • Flexibility: Easy to extend with different architectures, priors, and objectives

2. Probabilistic Framework

Consider a dataset \(\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N\) of i.i.d. samples from an unknown data distribution \(p^*(x)\). We assume a latent variable model: each observation \(x\) is generated from an unobserved latent variable \(z\):

\[ z \sim p(z), \quad x \sim p_\theta(x|z) \]

Here, \(p(z)\) is the prior over latent variables (typically the isotropic Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\)), and \(p_\theta(x|z)\) is the likelihood or decoder, parameterized by a neural network with parameters \(\theta\).

2.1 The Intractability Problem

Our goal is to learn \(\theta\) by maximizing the marginal log-likelihood:

\[ \log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x|z) p(z) \, dz \]

This integral is intractable for two reasons:

  1. We cannot enumerate all possible \(z\) values (continuous, high-dimensional)
  2. For most \(z\), the likelihood \(p_\theta(x|z)\) is nearly zero, making naive Monte Carlo estimation inefficient

Similarly, the true posterior \(p_\theta(z|x) = p_\theta(x|z)p(z)/p_\theta(x)\) is intractable because it requires computing \(p_\theta(x)\).

2.2 Amortized Variational Inference

VAEs address intractability by introducing an inference network (or encoder) \(q_\phi(z|x)\) that approximates the true posterior. This is called amortized inference because a single network (with parameters \(\phi\)) is trained to perform inference for all data points, rather than optimizing separate variational parameters for each \(x\).

Definition: VAE Components
  • Encoder \(q_\phi(z|x)\): Maps data to approximate posterior distribution (recognition model)
  • Decoder \(p_\theta(x|z)\): Maps latent codes to data distribution (generative model)
  • Prior \(p(z)\): Distribution over latent space (typically \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\))

The encoder typically outputs the parameters of a Gaussian distribution:

\[ q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(z; \mu_\phi(x), \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\phi(x))) \]

where \(\mu_\phi(x)\) and \(\sigma^2_\phi(x)\) are outputs of neural networks.

3. The Evidence Lower Bound

The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) provides a tractable objective for training VAEs. We derive it starting from the log marginal likelihood.

3.1 Derivation of ELBO

Starting with the log-likelihood:

\[ \log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x, z) \, dz = \log \int p_\theta(x|z) p(z) \, dz \]

We introduce the approximate posterior \(q_\phi(z|x)\) by multiplying and dividing:

\[ \log p_\theta(x) = \log \int q_\phi(z|x) \frac{p_\theta(x|z) p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)} \, dz \]

Applying Jensen's inequality (since \(\log\) is concave):

\[ \log p_\theta(x) \geq \int q_\phi(z|x) \log \frac{p_\theta(x|z) p(z)}{q_\phi(z|x)} \, dz \]

Expanding the logarithm:

\[ \log p_\theta(x) \geq \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction term}} - \underbrace{D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))}_{\text{regularization term}} \]

This lower bound is the ELBO.

Theorem: ELBO Decomposition

The log-evidence can be decomposed exactly as:

\[ \log p_\theta(x) = \text{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) + D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p_\theta(z|x)) \]

Since KL divergence is non-negative, ELBO \(\leq \log p_\theta(x)\). The bound is tight if and only if \(q_\phi(z|x) = p_\theta(z|x)\), i.e., when the approximate posterior equals the true posterior.

3.2 Interpretation of ELBO Terms

The ELBO has two intuitive terms with complementary roles:

  1. Reconstruction Term: \(\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]\)

    Measures how well the decoder can reconstruct \(x\) from latent samples drawn from the encoder. Maximizing this term encourages the encoder to preserve information about \(x\) in \(z\), and the decoder to accurately reconstruct \(x\) from \(z\).

  2. Regularization Term: \(-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))\)

    Encourages the approximate posterior \(q_\phi(z|x)\) to stay close to the prior \(p(z)\). This regularization ensures the latent space has the structure needed for generation: we can sample \(z \sim p(z)\) and decode it to generate new data.

The Reconstruction-Regularization Trade-off

These two terms are in tension: perfect reconstruction might require posteriors that deviate significantly from the prior, while perfect regularization (matching the prior exactly) would mean the latent code carries no information about the input. The ELBO balances these competing objectives.

3.3 Closed-Form KL for Gaussians

When the posterior is Gaussian \(q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \text{diag}(\sigma^2))\) and the prior is standard Gaussian \(p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), the KL divergence has a closed-form solution:

\[ D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z)) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left( \mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1 \right) \]

where \(d\) is the latent dimension. This can be computed analytically without sampling.

4. The Reparameterization Trick

A key challenge in training VAEs is computing gradients of the reconstruction term \(\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]\) with respect to encoder parameters \(\phi\). The expectation involves sampling \(z \sim q_\phi(z|x)\), which is a stochastic operation that blocks gradient flow.

4.1 The Problem with Direct Sampling

If we sample \(z\) directly from \(q_\phi(z|x)\), we cannot compute \(\nabla_\phi\) because the sampling operation is not differentiable. The gradient \(\nabla_\phi z\) is undefined when \(z\) is obtained by sampling from a distribution parameterized by \(\phi\).

4.2 The Reparameterization Solution

The reparameterization trick expresses the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and an auxiliary noise variable:

\[ z = g_\phi(\epsilon, x) = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

Now the randomness is "externalized" to \(\epsilon\), which does not depend on \(\phi\). The function \(g_\phi\) is differentiable with respect to \(\phi\), enabling standard backpropagation:

\[ \nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}[\nabla_\phi f(g_\phi(\epsilon, x))] \]

In practice, we estimate this expectation with a single sample of \(\epsilon\) per data point.

Why Not REINFORCE?

An alternative is the REINFORCE (score function) gradient estimator:

\[ \nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z) \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z|x)] \]

This applies to any distribution but has high variance, requiring many samples or complex variance reduction techniques (baselines, control variates). The reparameterization trick provides low-variance gradients with just one sample, making training dramatically more efficient.

4.3 Applicability

The reparameterization trick requires that we can express samples from \(q_\phi(z|x)\) as a differentiable transformation of noise. This works for:

  • Gaussians (as shown above)
  • Any location-scale family
  • Distributions with differentiable inverse CDF

It does not directly apply to discrete distributions, which motivated techniques like the Gumbel-Softmax (for categorical) and VQ-VAE (for codebook learning).

5. Beta-VAE and Disentanglement

Beta-VAE (Higgins et al., 2017) modifies the VAE objective by introducing a hyperparameter \(\beta > 1\) that increases the weight of the KL term:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\beta = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z)) \]

When \(\beta = 1\), we recover the standard VAE. When \(\beta > 1\), we impose stronger regularization on the latent space.

5.1 Information Bottleneck Perspective

The KL term \(D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))\) upper-bounds the mutual information \(I(x; z)\) between data and latent codes. With \(\beta > 1\), we create a tighter information bottleneck: less information about \(x\) can pass through \(z\).

This forces the model to prioritize: it must preserve the most essential information for reconstruction while discarding irrelevant details. The hypothesis is that essential information corresponds to the true generative factors.

5.2 Disentanglement

A disentangled representation is one where each latent dimension corresponds to an independent factor of variation in the data. For example, in face images, separate dimensions might control:

  • Pose (viewing angle)
  • Lighting direction
  • Expression (smile, frown)
  • Identity features

The intuition for why \(\beta > 1\) promotes disentanglement:

  1. The factorial prior \(p(z) = \prod_j p(z_j)\) has independent dimensions
  2. Stronger KL penalty forces \(q_\phi(z|x)\) toward this factorial structure
  3. The information bottleneck forces the model to use capacity efficiently
  4. Axis-aligned, interpretable representations emerge as the most efficient encoding

5.3 The Reconstruction-Disentanglement Trade-off

Increasing \(\beta\) improves disentanglement but hurts reconstruction quality. With very large \(\beta\), the model may collapse to the prior and ignore the input entirely.

Practical Guidance
  • \(\beta = 1\): Standard VAE, best reconstruction
  • \(\beta \in [2, 10]\): Good balance for disentanglement experiments
  • \(\beta > 10\): Strong disentanglement, but often blurry reconstructions

6. Conditional VAE

Conditional VAE (CVAE) (Sohn et al., 2015) extends VAE to model conditional distributions \(p(x|c)\), where \(c\) is some conditioning information such as a class label, text description, or partial observation.

6.1 Generative Model

The conditional generative process is:

\[ z \sim p(z|c), \quad x \sim p_\theta(x|z, c) \]

The marginal becomes:

\[ p_\theta(x|c) = \int p_\theta(x|z, c) \, p(z|c) \, dz \]

6.2 Conditional ELBO

With an inference network \(q_\phi(z|x,c)\) that takes both \(x\) and \(c\) as input, the ELBO becomes:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x, c) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x,c)}[\log p_\theta(x|z,c)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x,c) \| p(z|c)) \]

6.3 Prior Choice

Two common choices for the conditional prior \(p(z|c)\):

  1. Condition-independent prior: \(p(z|c) = p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\)

    Simple but may not capture condition-specific structure.

  2. Learned conditional prior: \(p_\psi(z|c) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\psi(c), \sigma^2_\psi(c))\)

    More expressive; the prior adapts to each condition.

6.4 Conditioning Mechanisms

Several architectural approaches for incorporating condition \(c\):

  • Concatenation: Append \(c\) (or its embedding) to encoder/decoder inputs. Simple and effective.
  • Adaptive normalization (e.g., FiLM, AdaIN): Use \(c\) to modulate normalization layer parameters.
  • Cross-attention: Attend to condition embeddings. Common for sequence or text conditioning.

7. VQ-VAE: Discrete Latent Spaces

Vector Quantized VAE (VQ-VAE) (van den Oord et al., 2017) replaces the continuous Gaussian latent space with a discrete codebook. This enables modeling with powerful autoregressive priors and avoids the posterior collapse problem.

7.1 The Codebook

VQ-VAE maintains a codebook (embedding dictionary) \(E = \{e_1, \ldots, e_K\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) of \(K\) embedding vectors. The encoder outputs a continuous representation \(z_e(x)\), which is then quantized by finding the nearest codebook entry:

\[ z_q(x) = e_k, \quad \text{where } k = \arg\min_j \|z_e(x) - e_j\|_2 \]

The decoder receives \(z_q\) rather than \(z_e\). This discrete bottleneck forces the model to learn a compact, structured representation.

7.2 Straight-Through Estimator

The \(\arg\min\) operation is non-differentiable. VQ-VAE uses the straight-through estimator: in the forward pass, we use the quantized \(z_q\); in the backward pass, gradients flow directly to \(z_e\) as if \(z_q = z_e\).

This can be implemented as:

\[ z_q = z_e + \text{sg}(z_q - z_e) \]

where \(\text{sg}(\cdot)\) denotes stop-gradient (identity in forward, zero gradient in backward).

7.3 Loss Function

The VQ-VAE loss has three components:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{-\log p(x|z_q)}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \underbrace{\|\text{sg}(z_e) - z_q\|_2^2}_{\text{codebook loss}} + \underbrace{\beta \|z_e - \text{sg}(z_q)\|_2^2}_{\text{commitment loss}} \]
  • Reconstruction: Negative log-likelihood of data given quantized latents
  • Codebook loss: Moves codebook entries toward encoder outputs (only updates \(E\))
  • Commitment loss: Encourages encoder to commit to codebook entries (only updates encoder)

Alternatively, codebook entries can be updated using exponential moving average (EMA) of encoder outputs, which often works better in practice.

7.4 Advantages of Discrete Latents

  • No posterior collapse: No KL term that can be minimized to zero
  • Powerful priors: Can model with autoregressive transformers over discrete codes
  • Natural for discrete data: Good interface for text, symbolic data, discrete actions
  • Compression: Discrete codes can be directly stored/transmitted
Codebook Collapse

A common failure mode is codebook collapse: only a few codebook entries are used while others remain "dead." Solutions include: EMA updates, codebook reset (reinitialize dead entries), and auxiliary diversity losses.

8. Hierarchical VAEs

Hierarchical VAEs use multiple levels of latent variables to capture structure at different scales. This enables modeling of complex, multi-scale data distributions and has led to state-of-the-art image generation results.

8.1 Motivation

Standard VAEs use a single latent layer, which limits their expressiveness. Complex data often has hierarchical structure: images have global composition (layout, objects) and local details (textures, edges). A hierarchical latent space can naturally capture these different levels of abstraction.

8.2 Generative Model

Let \(z = (z_1, \ldots, z_L)\) be a hierarchy where \(z_L\) is the "top" (most abstract) level and \(z_1\) is closest to the data. The joint distribution factorizes top-down:

\[ p_\theta(x, z_{1:L}) = p(z_L) \prod_{l=1}^{L-1} p_\theta(z_l | z_{l+1:L}) \cdot p_\theta(x | z_{1:L}) \]

Higher levels capture global, semantic information (e.g., object identity, scene layout); lower levels capture local details (e.g., textures, precise edges).

8.3 Inference Model

The approximate posterior typically factorizes bottom-up:

\[ q_\phi(z_{1:L} | x) = \prod_{l=1}^{L} q_\phi(z_l | z_{1:l-1}, x) \]

8.4 The Posterior Collapse Problem

A major challenge in hierarchical VAEs is posterior collapse: upper latent layers may be ignored if lower layers are sufficiently expressive. The model learns to route all information through \(z_1\), and higher layers collapse to the prior.

Solutions that have proven effective:

  • Top-down inference: Let the inference network also receive top-down information, so higher layers can influence lower layer posteriors
  • Residual parameterization: Parameterize each level's prior as a residual from the higher level, ensuring each level must contribute
  • KL annealing: Gradually increase KL weight during training
  • Free bits: Allow a minimum KL per dimension before penalizing, ensuring each dimension carries some information
  • Architectural constraints: Limit the capacity of lower levels so they cannot capture everything alone

9. VAE-GAN

VAE-generated images tend to be blurry. This is because pixel-wise reconstruction losses (MSE, BCE) penalize any deviation from the target equally, regardless of whether it's perceptually important. The optimal solution under MSE is the mean of possible outputs, which is blurry when there's uncertainty.

9.1 Why VAEs Produce Blurry Images

Consider reconstructing a face: there are many valid reconstructions that differ in fine details (exact hair strand positions, skin texture). Under MSE loss, the optimal decoder output is the average of all these possibilities—a blurry image.

9.2 Feature-Space Reconstruction

VAE-GAN (Larsen et al., 2016) addresses this by replacing pixel-wise reconstruction with feature matching loss. Instead of comparing pixels, we compare features extracted by a discriminator network \(D\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}} = \|f_D(x) - f_D(\hat{x})\|_2^2 \]

where \(f_D\) extracts intermediate layer activations from the discriminator. This loss is more perceptually meaningful: images that look similar to humans also have similar discriminator features.

9.3 Combined Objective

VAE-GAN training involves three networks with interleaved updates:

  • Encoder: Minimizes feature reconstruction loss + KL divergence
  • Decoder/Generator: Minimizes feature reconstruction + adversarial loss (fool discriminator)
  • Discriminator: Maximizes ability to distinguish real from reconstructed/generated images
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE-GAN}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}} + \beta \cdot D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z)) + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}} \]

The discriminator must distinguish between three types of images: real images, reconstructed images (from encoding real data), and samples (from the prior).

Training Considerations

VAE-GAN training can be unstable due to the adversarial component. Practical tips: use separate learning rates for different networks, employ spectral normalization in the discriminator, consider two-timescale update rules, and monitor for mode collapse or discriminator overfitting.

10. Connections to Other Models

10.1 VAE and Deterministic Autoencoders

Without the KL regularization term (or as \(\sigma \to 0\)), a VAE becomes a deterministic autoencoder. The latent space may then have "holes"— regions where no training data maps—making sampling unreliable. The KL term ensures the latent space is "filled in" and suitable for generation.

10.2 VAE and Normalizing Flows

The Gaussian posterior \(q_\phi(z|x)\) is often too simple to match complex true posteriors. Normalizing flows can enhance VAEs by transforming a simple base distribution through a sequence of invertible mappings:

\[ z_K = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1(z_0), \quad z_0 \sim q_0(z_0|x) \]

This gives a more expressive posterior while maintaining tractable density evaluation.

10.3 VAE and Diffusion Models

Diffusion models can be viewed as hierarchical VAEs with special structure:

  • Infinitely many latent levels (continuous time)
  • Fixed (non-learned) forward process
  • Markovian structure in both forward and reverse processes

Both VAEs and diffusion models optimize variational bounds on the log-likelihood, revealing their deep connection.

10.4 VAE and Energy-Based Models

VAEs with expressive priors (e.g., energy-based priors) connect to energy-based models. The prior \(p(z) \propto \exp(-E(z))\) defines an energy landscape in latent space. This enables modeling of more complex latent structure but requires MCMC sampling during training.
