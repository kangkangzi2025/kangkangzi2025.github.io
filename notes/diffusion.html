1. Introduction

Diffusion models, also known as score-based generative models, have emerged as the dominant paradigm for generative modeling. They power state-of-the-art systems including Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 3, Midjourney, and OpenAI's Sora. The core idea is remarkably elegant: gradually destroy data by adding noise, then learn to reverse this corruption process.

The theoretical foundations were established through two parallel lines of research: denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM) by Ho et al. (2020), building on earlier work by Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015), and score matching with Langevin dynamics by Song & Ermon (2019). Song et al. (2021) unified these perspectives through stochastic differential equations (SDEs), revealing them as two views of the same underlying framework.

Key Insight

If we can learn to reverse small corruptions, we can start from pure noise and iteratively denoise to generate realistic data. The forward corruption process is fixed and trivial; all the modeling power lies in learning the reverse.

Why have diffusion models become so successful?

  • Stable training: Unlike GANs, there's no adversarial min-max game that can diverge
  • Mode coverage: Unlike GANs, diffusion models don't suffer from mode collapse
  • Theoretical foundation: Clear connection to variational inference and score matching
  • Flexible conditioning: Natural mechanisms for text, class, and structural guidance
  • High quality: State-of-the-art sample quality across images, audio, video, and 3D

The main drawback is slow sampling: generation requires hundreds to thousands of sequential denoising steps. This has motivated extensive research into accelerated sampling methods, including DDIM, consistency models, and distillation techniques.

2. The Forward Diffusion Process

The forward process defines a Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data over \(T\) timesteps. Starting from a data point \(x_0 \sim q(x_0)\), we construct a sequence \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T\) where each step adds a small amount of noise:

Definition: Forward Process

The forward process is defined as:

\[ q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \, x_{t-1}, \beta_t I) \]

where \(\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T\) is the noise schedule with \(\beta_t \in (0, 1)\). The full forward process is:

\[ q(x_{1:T} | x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t | x_{t-1}) \]

The variance schedule \(\{\beta_t\}\) controls how quickly information is destroyed. Small \(\beta_t\) values (e.g., \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\)) ensure the transition between adjacent timesteps is nearly Gaussian, which is crucial for the reverse process.

2.1 Closed-Form Marginal

A crucial property of the forward process is that we can sample \(x_t\) directly from \(x_0\) without iterating through intermediate steps. This is essential for efficient training.

Theorem: Closed-Form Sampling

Let \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\) and \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s\). Then:

\[ q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \, x_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) I) \]

Equivalently, using the reparameterization trick:

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \, \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

Proof sketch: By induction, using the fact that the sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian. At step \(t\):

\[ x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t} x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \bar{\epsilon}_{t-1}) + \sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon_t \]

Combining the noise terms (using variance addition for independent Gaussians) yields the result.

2.2 Noise Schedules

The choice of \(\{\beta_t\}\) significantly affects generation quality. As \(t \to T\), we want \(\bar{\alpha}_T \approx 0\) so that \(x_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)\)—pure noise with no information about \(x_0\).

Linear schedule (original DDPM):

\[ \beta_t = \beta_{\min} + \frac{t-1}{T-1}(\beta_{\max} - \beta_{\min}) \]

Typically \(\beta_{\min} = 10^{-4}\), \(\beta_{\max} = 0.02\), and \(T = 1000\).

Cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021):

Designed so that \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) decreases smoothly from 1 to 0. The cosine schedule avoids the problem that linear schedules destroy too much information early in the process and too little near the end. It's defined implicitly through:

\[ \bar{\alpha}_t = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \quad f(t) = \cos\left(\frac{t/T + s}{1 + s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2}\right)^2 \]

where \(s\) is a small offset (typically 0.008) to prevent \(\beta_t\) from being too small at \(t = 0\).

Practical Guidance: Schedule Selection
  • Linear: Simple, works well for most image generation tasks
  • Cosine: Often better for high-resolution images, preserves more structure longer
  • Scaled linear: Adjusted for latent diffusion where latent variance differs from pixel variance

3. The Reverse Process

To generate data, we need to reverse the forward process: start from \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and progressively denoise to obtain \(x_0\). The key insight is that for small \(\beta_t\), the reverse conditional \(q(x_{t-1} | x_t)\) is also approximately Gaussian.

3.1 The Reverse Posterior

While \(q(x_{t-1} | x_t)\) is intractable (it depends on the entire data distribution), \(q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0)\) has a closed form:

Theorem: Reverse Posterior

The posterior \(q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0)\) is Gaussian:

\[ q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0), \tilde{\beta}_t I) \]

where:

\[ \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} x_t \] \[ \tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t \]

Proof: Apply Bayes' rule: \(q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t | x_{t-1}) q(x_{t-1} | x_0)\). Both terms on the right are Gaussian, so their product is Gaussian. The mean and variance follow from completing the square.

3.2 Learning the Reverse Process

Since we don't know \(x_0\) during generation, we learn a neural network \(p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)\) to approximate \(q(x_{t-1} | x_t)\). This is parameterized as:

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_\theta(x_t, t), \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t)) \]

In practice, \(\Sigma_\theta\) is often fixed to \(\beta_t I\) or \(\tilde{\beta}_t I\), leaving only \(\mu_\theta\) to be learned. There are several equivalent parameterizations for the mean:

  1. Predict \(\mu\) directly: The network outputs \(\mu_\theta(x_t, t)\)
  2. Predict \(x_0\): The network outputs \(\hat{x}_0 = f_\theta(x_t, t)\), then compute \(\mu\) using the formula for \(\tilde{\mu}_t\)
  3. Predict \(\epsilon\) (the standard choice): The network outputs \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) predicting the noise that was added. Since \(x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon) / \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\), this uniquely determines \(\mu_\theta\)
Why Predict Noise?

Predicting \(\epsilon\) has been found to work best empirically. Intuitively, the noise \(\epsilon\) has constant variance across timesteps, while \(x_0\) becomes increasingly uncertain as \(t\) grows. The \(\epsilon\)-prediction provides a more stable learning signal.

4. Training Objective

Diffusion models are trained by maximizing a variational lower bound (ELBO) on the log-likelihood. The derivation closely parallels that of VAEs.

4.1 Variational Lower Bound

Starting from the marginal log-likelihood:

\[ \log p_\theta(x_0) = \log \int p_\theta(x_{0:T}) \, dx_{1:T} \]

Introducing the forward process as a variational distribution:

\[ \log p_\theta(x_0) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)} \left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}|x_0)} \right] = -\mathcal{L}_{VLB} \]

The VLB can be decomposed into interpretable terms:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{VLB} = \underbrace{D_{KL}(q(x_T|x_0) \| p(x_T))}_{\mathcal{L}_T} + \sum_{t=2}^{T} \underbrace{D_{KL}(q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0) \| p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t))}_{\mathcal{L}_{t-1}} - \underbrace{\log p_\theta(x_0|x_1)}_{\mathcal{L}_0} \]
  • \(\mathcal{L}_T\): Prior matching term. Since both \(q(x_T|x_0)\) and \(p(x_T)\) are near \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) for large \(T\), this is approximately zero.
  • \(\mathcal{L}_{t-1}\): Denoising matching terms. The model must match the true reverse posteriors.
  • \(\mathcal{L}_0\): Reconstruction term, typically modeled with a discretized decoder.

4.2 The Simplified Objective

Ho et al. (2020) showed that a simplified objective works better in practice:

Definition: DDPM Training Objective
\[ \mathcal{L}_{simple} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}(1,T), x_0 \sim q(x_0), \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)} \left[ \| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \|^2 \right] \]

where \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon\).

This is simply mean squared error between predicted and actual noise! The network is trained as a denoiser for all noise levels simultaneously, with \(t\) sampled uniformly.

Why does this work? The simplified objective is a reweighted version of the VLB. It places equal weight on all timesteps, which empirically produces better sample quality than the VLB weighting (which emphasizes early timesteps where reconstruction error is small).

5. Score Matching Perspective

An alternative and illuminating view comes from score matching. The score function of a distribution \(p(x)\) is the gradient of its log-density:

\[ s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x) \]

5.1 Score Function Intuition

The score points in the direction of increasing probability. If we can estimate the score, we can generate samples using Langevin dynamics:

\[ x_{i+1} = x_i + \frac{\eta}{2} \nabla_x \log p(x_i) + \sqrt{\eta} \, z_i, \quad z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

As \(\eta \to 0\) and the number of steps \(\to \infty\), samples from this chain converge to samples from \(p(x)\).

5.2 Noise Conditional Score

Direct score estimation fails in practice because the data distribution has low support in high-dimensional space. The key insight of Song & Ermon (2019) is to estimate scores of noise-perturbed data distributions \(q_\sigma(x) = \int q(x') \mathcal{N}(x; x', \sigma^2 I) dx'\).

For Gaussian perturbation, the score of the noisy distribution has a beautiful form:

Theorem: Score-Noise Relationship
\[ \nabla_x \log q_\sigma(x) = -\frac{x - \mathbb{E}[x_0 | x]}{\sigma^2} = -\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma} \]

where \(\epsilon\) is the noise that was added to \(x_0\) to obtain \(x\). Thus, predicting noise is equivalent to predicting the score (up to scaling).

5.3 Unified SDE Framework

Song et al. (2021) unified discrete-time diffusion and score matching through stochastic differential equations (SDEs). The forward process is described by:

\[ dx = f(x, t) \, dt + g(t) \, dw \]

where \(f\) is the drift, \(g\) is the diffusion coefficient, and \(w\) is a Wiener process. Remarkably, there exists a reverse-time SDE:

\[ dx = [f(x, t) - g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x)] \, dt + g(t) \, d\bar{w} \]

where \(\bar{w}\) is a reverse-time Wiener process and \(p_t(x)\) is the marginal density at time \(t\). This reveals that the score function \(\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\) is all we need to reverse the diffusion.

The Probability Flow ODE

For any diffusion SDE, there exists a deterministic ODE with identical marginal densities at all times:

\[ dx = \left[ f(x, t) - \frac{1}{2} g(t)^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x) \right] dt \]

This probability flow ODE enables deterministic sampling (used in DDIM) and exact likelihood computation via the change of variables formula.

6. Accelerated Sampling: DDIM

Standard DDPM sampling requires \(T \approx 1000\) steps, which is slow. Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) by Song et al. (2020) enable faster sampling with far fewer steps using the same trained model.

6.1 Key Insight

The forward marginals \(q(x_t | x_0)\) are uniquely defined, but there are many joint distributions \(q(x_{1:T} | x_0)\) that yield the same marginals. DDPM uses a specific Markovian joint, but we can choose a different, non-Markovian process that permits larger jumps.

6.2 DDIM Update Rule

Definition: DDIM Sampling

Given the predicted noise \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\), the DDIM update is:

\[ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \underbrace{\left( \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \, \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}} \right)}_{\text{predicted } x_0} + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2} \, \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) + \sigma_t \epsilon \]

where \(\sigma_t\) controls stochasticity. Setting \(\sigma_t = 0\) gives deterministic sampling.

Crucially, DDIM can use a subsequence of timesteps (e.g., \(\{100, 200, 300, \ldots, 1000\}\) instead of all 1000). With 50-100 steps, DDIM produces samples comparable to 1000-step DDPM.

6.3 Deterministic Generation

When \(\sigma_t = 0\), DDIM is deterministic: the same initial noise \(x_T\) always produces the same output \(x_0\). This enables:

  • Semantic interpolation: Interpolate in noise space for smooth transitions
  • Inversion: Given \(x_0\), find the \(x_T\) that produces it (useful for editing)
  • Consistent reconstruction: Deterministic mapping from noise to image

7. Latent Diffusion Models

Operating diffusion in pixel space is computationally expensive: a 512×512×3 image has 786,432 dimensions. Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) by Rombach et al. (2022) perform diffusion in a compressed latent space, dramatically reducing computational cost while maintaining quality.

7.1 Two-Stage Approach

LDM consists of two decoupled stages:

  1. Perceptual compression: Train an autoencoder (typically VQ-VAE or KL-regularized VAE) to compress images \(x\) to latents \(z = \mathcal{E}(x)\) and reconstruct \(\hat{x} = \mathcal{D}(z)\).
  2. Latent diffusion: Train a diffusion model in the latent space \(z\). The compression ratio is typically 8× (e.g., 512×512×3 → 64×64×4), yielding a 48× reduction in dimensionality.

7.2 Why Latent Space Works

The key insight is that perceptual quality depends primarily on semantic content, not pixel-level details. The autoencoder removes imperceptible high-frequency details while preserving semantically important structure. Diffusion then operates only on this semantically meaningful latent space.

Autoencoder Requirements

The autoencoder must be trained with perceptual losses (comparing features from pretrained networks like VGG) and adversarial losses to ensure sharp reconstructions. Simple MSE loss produces blurry results.

7.3 Text Conditioning via Cross-Attention

LDM introduced cross-attention as the primary mechanism for conditioning on text. The denoising U-Net contains attention layers where:

  • Query (\(Q\)): Comes from spatial image features
  • Key (\(K\)) and Value (\(V\)): Come from text embeddings (e.g., from CLIP or T5)
\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V \]

This allows each spatial location in the image to attend to relevant parts of the text prompt, enabling fine-grained text-image alignment.

8. Conditioning and Guidance

Controlling diffusion models with conditions (text, class labels, images) is essential for practical applications. Two main approaches exist: classifier guidance and classifier-free guidance.

8.1 Classifier Guidance

Dhariwal & Nichol (2021) showed that a pretrained classifier \(p_\phi(y | x_t)\) can guide generation toward class \(y\). Using Bayes' rule on scores:

\[ \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t | y) = \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t) + \nabla_{x_t} \log p(y | x_t) \]

The modified score adds the classifier gradient, pushing samples toward regions classified as \(y\):

\[ \tilde{\epsilon}_\theta = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot s \cdot \nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(y | x_t) \]

where \(s > 0\) is the guidance scale. Higher \(s\) means stronger conditioning but reduced diversity.

8.2 Classifier-Free Guidance

Classifier guidance requires training a separate classifier on noisy images. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) by Ho & Salimans (2022) achieves similar effects without any classifier.

Definition: Classifier-Free Guidance

Training: Randomly drop the condition \(c\) with probability \(p_{uncond}\) (typically 10%), replacing it with a null token \(\emptyset\). This trains a single model for both conditional \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)\) and unconditional \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \emptyset)\) generation.

Sampling: Extrapolate away from the unconditional direction:

\[ \tilde{\epsilon}_\theta = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \emptyset) + w \cdot (\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, \emptyset)) \]

where \(w > 1\) is the guidance scale (typically 7-12 for text-to-image).

CFG has become the standard approach due to its simplicity and effectiveness. It can be interpreted as moving the score estimate further in the "conditional direction" relative to the unconditional baseline.

8.3 Structural Conditioning: ControlNet

While text controls semantics, it struggles with precise spatial structure. ControlNet (Zhang et al., 2023) adds structural conditions (edges, depth maps, poses) by:

  1. Copying the encoder of a pretrained U-Net
  2. Adding zero-initialized convolutions to connect the copy to the original
  3. Training only the copy on paired (image, structure) data

The zero initialization ensures the pretrained model is preserved at the start of training. The locked original model provides strong image priors while the trainable copy learns to inject structural control.

9. Diffusion Transformers

The U-Net architecture dominated early diffusion models, but Transformers offer advantages in scaling behavior. Diffusion Transformer (DiT) by Peebles & Xie (2023) showed that Transformers can match or exceed U-Net performance while following predictable scaling laws.

9.1 From U-Net to Transformer

Aspect U-Net DiT
Input processing Convolutions Patchify (like ViT)
Core operations ResBlocks + Attention Transformer blocks
Multi-scale Encoder-decoder with skip connections Single scale (all patches)
Conditioning Cross-attention, adaptive norm AdaLN (Adaptive LayerNorm)
Scaling Complex (many hyperparameters) Simple (depth, width, heads)

9.2 AdaLN-Zero

DiT conditions on timestep and class label by modulating LayerNorm parameters. The condition embedding produces scale (\(\gamma\)), shift (\(\beta\)), and gate (\(\alpha\)) parameters:

\[ \text{AdaLN}(x, c) = \gamma(c) \cdot \text{LayerNorm}(x) + \beta(c) \]

The "Zero" refers to initializing the final linear layer to output zeros. This makes each DiT block an identity function at initialization, which stabilizes training of deep networks (similar to ReZero and other techniques).

9.3 Scaling Laws

A key finding of DiT is that diffusion Transformers follow scaling laws similar to language models: performance improves predictably with increased compute, model size, and data. This makes DiT architectures attractive for large-scale training, as exemplified by systems like Sora.

DiT Scaling Results

On ImageNet 256×256, DiT-XL/2 (675M parameters) achieved FID 2.27, outperforming prior diffusion models. Larger models consistently yield better FID, with the scaling trend showing no signs of saturation.

10. Video Diffusion Models

Extending diffusion to video introduces the challenge of temporal consistency: objects must move smoothly and maintain their identity across frames. Video diffusion models must jointly model spatial content and temporal dynamics.

10.1 Temporal Modeling Strategies

Several architectural approaches have been explored:

  1. 3D Convolutions: Replace 2D convolutions with 3D (sometimes called "inflating" a 2D model). Captures local spatio-temporal patterns.
  2. Temporal Attention: Add attention across the time dimension, allowing frames to communicate. Often used with "factorized" attention that separates spatial and temporal attention for efficiency.
  3. Causal vs. Bidirectional: Causal attention (each frame sees only past frames) enables autoregressive generation; bidirectional attention allows global temporal reasoning but requires all frames to be generated together.

10.2 Video Compression

Videos are even more computationally demanding than images. Video VAEs compress both spatially and temporally. For example, compressing 64 frames at 256×256 by 8× spatially and 4× temporally yields 16×32×32 latents—a massive reduction enabling practical training and inference.

10.3 Training Strategies

Video data is scarcer than image data, so several techniques help:

  • Image pretraining + video finetuning: Train spatial layers on images, then add and train temporal layers on video (with spatial layers frozen or at low learning rate)
  • Joint image-video training: Train on both images (as single-frame videos) and videos, leveraging abundant image data
  • Progressive training: Start with low resolution and few frames, gradually increase both during training

10.4 Modern Architectures: Sora

OpenAI's Sora (2024) represents the current frontier. Based on the technical report, key innovations include:

  • Spacetime patches: 3D patches that jointly capture spatial and temporal information
  • DiT for video: Transformer architecture scaled to massive size
  • Variable resolution/duration: Training on native aspect ratios and lengths, avoiding cropping
  • Emergent capabilities: At scale, Sora demonstrates 3D consistency, object permanence, and basic physics understanding
Open Source Alternatives
  • Open-Sora: Community effort to replicate Sora architecture
  • CogVideoX: Tsinghua/Zhipu's open model with detailed technical report, featuring 3D VAE and Mixture of Experts

11. Connections to Other Models

11.1 Diffusion and VAEs

Diffusion models can be viewed as hierarchical VAEs with a specific structure:

  • Infinitely many (or very many) latent levels corresponding to timesteps
  • Fixed, non-learned forward process (the "encoder")
  • Markovian structure in both directions
  • Gaussian transitions throughout

Both optimize variational bounds on log-likelihood. The ELBO derivation for diffusion models is essentially the hierarchical VAE ELBO with the specific forward process constraints.

11.2 Diffusion and Energy-Based Models

Score matching connects diffusion to energy-based models (EBMs). An EBM defines \(p(x) \propto \exp(-E_\theta(x))\), with score \(\nabla_x \log p(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x)\). Score-based diffusion models essentially learn a sequence of energy functions (one per noise level) without explicit normalization.

11.3 Diffusion and Normalizing Flows

The probability flow ODE reveals connections to continuous normalizing flows (CNFs). Both define deterministic transformations from simple distributions to data distributions. Diffusion models can be seen as CNFs where the velocity field is constrained to depend on the score, which is learned via denoising.

11.4 Diffusion and GANs

While GANs and diffusion models both generate samples, they differ fundamentally:

  • GANs use adversarial training; diffusion uses likelihood-based training
  • GANs generate in one forward pass; diffusion requires iterative sampling
  • GANs often have mode collapse; diffusion typically covers all modes
  • GANs have unstable training; diffusion training is stable

These differences explain why diffusion has largely replaced GANs for high-quality generation, despite the sampling speed disadvantage.
